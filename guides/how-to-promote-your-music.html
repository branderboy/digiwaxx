How to Promote Your Music

How do I promote my music so people actually hear it?

Quick Answer

Promote your music in this order: finish and package the record, set a release date and plan backward from it, put the song in front of people who already play music for audiences (DJs, radio hosts, curators) before release week, post content around it the whole way through, then put money behind whatever is already reacting. Uploading is not promotion.

Most artists search this after the upload, when nothing happened. The fix is the same whether you call it promoting, marketing or advertising your music: borrow audiences that already exist, give them a reason to react, and make sure there is somewhere for that reaction to land.

How to Promote Your Music, Step by Step

  1. Package the record. Final master, clean and dirty versions, artwork, a short bio and one good photo. DJs and curators skip anything that is not ready to use. The EPK builder and bio generator get this done fast.
  2. Pick a date and work backward. Four to eight weeks of runway lets you deliver to your distributor, pitch editorial and line up everything below. The 60-day release plan dates every step.
  3. Get it to the people who play music for others. DJs, radio and mixshow hosts, and curators turn one play into a room or a show full of listeners. Record pool service reaches thousands of working DJs in one submission.
  4. Post around the record, not just about it. Short clips of the hook, the story behind the song, reactions from rooms where it played. Plan two or three weeks of content before release day.
  5. Concentrate release week. Everything lands in the same seven days: socials, DJ spins, radio, emails, so every platform sees a spike at once.
  6. Work the reaction for 60 days. Follow up with the DJs and outlets that responded, push the clips that worked, and fire a second wave. The after-release guide covers this part.

How to Market Your Music

Marketing is the plan; promotion is the push. Marketing your music means deciding who the song is for, what the artist looks and sounds like everywhere, and when each release lands. Write those three things down before you spend anything. Then every push, from a DJ campaign to a paid post, points at the same listener with the same story. The music marketing checklist puts it on one page.

How to Advertise Your Music

Paid ads work best as an amplifier, not a starting point. Run them after a song shows organic signal: a clip that outperforms your others, a city where DJs are playing it, a spike in saves. Then put a small budget behind that exact clip, aimed at that exact audience. Ads aimed at a song nobody has reacted to yet usually buy views that do not turn into fans. The release budget calculator helps you split the money.

How to Get Your Music Heard

If nobody is listening yet, do not try to build an audience from zero. Borrow one. DJs, radio hosts, playlist curators, blogs and creators already have people listening to them; your job is to get the record to them in a form they can use. That is the whole idea behind reaching DJs: one DJ playing your song in a full room is more listeners than most new uploads get in a month. If you are stuck here, read why nobody listens to my music.

Where to Post and Promote Your Music

  • Streaming services (Spotify, Apple Music, TIDAL): through a distributor, where most fans end up listening
  • SoundCloud and Audiomack: direct uploads with no distributor, good for early versions and DJ culture. See SoundCloud promotion and Audiomack promotion
  • YouTube: the video, a visualizer and Shorts. See YouTube music promotion
  • Short video (TikTok, Reels, Shorts): clips of the hook and the story, where most discovery starts
  • Record pools: where working DJs download new music
  • Blogs and press: once you have something to say, a press release helps. The press release generator drafts one

How to Promote Your Music on Spotify, SoundCloud, YouTube and Apple Music

Every platform rewards the same thing: people finding the song on their own and coming back to it. Each one has its own levers, from editorial pitching on Spotify to reposts on SoundCloud. The platform playbooks go through them one by one: Spotify, SoundCloud, YouTube, Apple Music and Audiomack. The demand that moves all of them usually starts off-platform, in rooms and on radio.

How to Promote a Song Before and After Release

Before release, the goal is anticipation: pre-saves, teasers, and the record already in DJs' hands. After release, the goal is momentum: following up the reaction and giving the song a second wave. Read how to build anticipation for the first half and the song rollout timeline for the whole arc.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best way to promote your music?

Get the song to people who already have listeners, like DJs, radio hosts and curators, before and during release week, and back it with steady content. Paid ads come after the song shows a reaction, not before.

How long should you promote a song?

Plan for about 60 days: two to four weeks before release, a concentrated release week, and four to five weeks working the reaction with a second wave.

Should I pay for music promotion?

Pay for access you cannot get on your own, such as DJ servicing or real curator pitching. Never pay for guaranteed streams, plays or playlist placements; they break platform rules and poison your data.

How do I get my music to DJs?

Package the record properly and send it through a record pool, which reaches thousands of working DJs in one submission. Digiwaxx has done that since 1998.
